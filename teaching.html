Teaching philosophy

I strive to create courses that engage, challenge, and motivate my students while preparing them for a range of job opportunities in the public and private sectors. My courses are grounded in rhetorical principles, user advocacy, industry and community engagement, and universal design. My teaching and research come together in courses devoted to web accessibility and disability studies but also through my abiding interest in making inclusivity and accessibility the centerpiece of my syllabi, assignments, and classroom spaces.

My courses reflect an expansive definition of technical and professional communication, fields of study that must prepare our undergraduate students not only to write well but to design information with a range of tools, genres, and modalities that meets users' needs. Technical and professional communicators must be able to create and blend words, speech and other sounds, images, and video into texts that address problems most effectively and reach the widest audience regardless of ability. They must also know how to transform data into infographics and other displays. My pedagogy is multimodal, offering students multiple means of representation, action, expression, and engagement (in keeping with universal design principles).

My students work closely with real community partners because I value community engagement to help them learn about project management, industry expectations, and teamwork. Technical communication is a bridge between users and clients. Client projects often raise difficult questions about the ethics of advocating for users when a client may have conflicting needs or ideas. These conflicts can become teachable moments that allow us to discuss the importance of ethics not in a vacuum but through real writing problems that students are grappling with. Working with community partners who are committed to diversity initiatives can be an effective way to promote social justice.

My pedagogy is grounded in rhetorical principles and user-centered design. The focus of my teaching at the undergraduate level is not on software tools but on helping students analyze rhetorical situations, develop solutions to users' needs, apply principles of good writing and design, and test documents with users. My concept of user-centered design in the classroom tends to go further than current notions by questioning narrow definitions of users that assume an able-bodied norm and leave out a wide range of users with disabilities. Accessibility is a mindset in my pedagogy rather than an add-on, and I strive to find ways to bake accessibility into my teaching and my syllabi from the start.

I locate technical communication firmly within the critical tradition of the humanities. My pedagogy is humanistic: critical, reflective, ethical, and rhetorical. Even as students learn to produce effective documents for industry and non-profit community partners, they are also challenged to be critical consumers and producers who question norms and values, including the stereotyped norm of the technical communicator as a glorified typist. Ideally, I want to give my students the tools to move one day into leadership positions. I believe a critical pedagogy will make good on the promise inherent in the humanities to produce an ethical, reflective workforce of leaders and project managers.

My vision of writing in a changing academy takes account of the increasingly multimodal and technological world in which students and their writing live. My courses locate writing within a larger multimodal matrix, a textual fusion of sights and sounds on the web, on their phones, in the instructional and informational YouTube videos they create and consume. We cannot continue to hold onto the outdated fantasy of the essay genre as the foundation of our writing courses. We should prepare our students to compose with and through technology, to think of writing as an integral component of multimodal composition, to analyze their audiences more critically and inclusively, and to situate writing as public expression for various audiences. It's an exciting and important time to be a professional writer, and we should be helping our students cultivate public voices that readers want to listen to.

Selected courses

I teach a range of courses at the intersection of technical writing, sound studies, inclusive design, and AI.

Undergraduate

Synthetic Rhetoric: Writing, Designing, and Creating with AI

Artificial intelligence isn't just reshaping how we write. It's transforming how we create, make, think, and learn. This course will explore the ethical questions we face as writers, readers, designers, artists, and critical thinkers in the age of generative AI. We will reconsider authorship, plagiarism, creativity, and the composing process itself. When does ChatGPT make us more creative and when does it provide a shortcut to learning and critical thinking? We'll also discuss the impacts of AI on the environment, the nature of work, and the transmission of culture and knowledge (including algorithmic bias). When should we (and how can we) resist and refuse AI? Which skills and jobs will remain "AI proof"?

In Fall 2025, this course culminated in AI Literacy Day on December 4th, a grant-funded collaboration with the UD library that brought guest speakers to campus and featured a showcase of students' best work in the class.

Unplugged: Reclaiming Our Agency in an Attention Economy

When so much of daily life is engineered to be clickable, scrollable, bingeable, and increasingly automated by AI, what would it take to reclaim our attention and rebuild a writing practice that supports close reading, deep listening, and sustained thinking? In this course, we'll explore how technical systems and mobile apps keep us hooked: dark patterns, infinite scroll, algorithmic feeds, and even chatbots that feel a little too helpful. How are our choices being shaped, what data are being extracted, and who profits when we can't look away from our devices? We'll also look at what the research says (and why studies sometimes disagree) about social media, smartphones, and wellbeing.

But we won't just critique technology. Students will build actual toolkits for learning and writing in an always-on world: a StudyTok-inspired study space, unplugged study protocols, a paper-based archive of ideas (a commonplace book), slow annotation and synthesis mapping, and focused drafting sprints using constraints and prototypes. Students will revise by physically reshaping structures—cutting, reordering, reverse outlining—and they'll practice attention-sharing through listening and peer response. To make these practices sustainable, students will also explore attention as something shaped by bodies and communities: how breath, posture, and movement affect composing (embodied writing), how listening can become a shared classroom craft (community writing), and how mindfulness must include critiques of power, access, and the attention economy (critical mindfulness).

Soundwriting: Composing Creatively and Rhetorically with Digital Audio

What does it mean to compose with sound, and in what ways is it rhetorically powerful? How can we leverage sound's unique affordances while building on the theories and practices we already know from writing studies? How do we ensure that our work remains accessible to everyone, regardless of ability or disability? This course values the myriad and hybrid ways we communicate meaning—writing, sound, image, and video—by offering theoretical and practical perspectives that expand our understanding of textuality beyond the printed page.

Drawing on scholarship from sound studies, sonic rhetoric, multimodal composition, and disability media studies, this course will examine how podcasts, soundscapes, recorded performances, and other audio texts convey meaning. Along the way, students will create short podcasts, audio essays, "soundwalk" reflections, and multimedia projects. They will gain hands-on experience with basic recording and editing tools and learn to produce accessible media (including transcripts and captions). By the end of the semester, students will sharpen their critical listening skills, develop innovative approaches to digital storytelling, and discover how sound can enrich—and even transform—traditional ideas of writing.

Technical Writing

This is my university's core service course for STEM students. It introduces students to technical communication genres and major concepts. To help students prepare for writing in professional contexts, this course focuses on common genres and styles of technical writing, including technical definitions, descriptions and instructions, informative materials, and research proposals.

This course helps students think like technical writers who can "translate" technical knowledge into user-centered forms of writing and visual design. We approach technical communication through three overlapping aims:

  • To inform (explain, describe, define, review literature, simplify or translate complex information, accommodate technologies to users)
  • To persuade (propose, recommend, argue, market, offer, change beliefs)
  • To instruct (teach, tutor, help accomplish tasks)

At the end of the course, students will have a better understanding of how professionals communicate technical ideas to diverse audiences.

Web Access for All

How do people with disabilities access electronic information? What do designers need to know to ensure their websites and other electronic documents are optimally accessible? What laws regulate the accessibility of electronic information? What responsibilities do we have as designers, working professionals, and technology users to the sizeable percentage of people in this country who are disabled? What theoretical and ethical perspectives (e.g. disability studies, universal design, aging studies) should motivate our efforts to create inclusive digital environments?

In this course, students will learn how to make digital content accessible to the widest population. We will explore several key web accessibility topics: closed captioning, video description, color contrast, screen readers, screen magnifiers, style sheets, web accessibility checkers, PDF accessibility, PowerPoint and Word accessibility, accessibility testing, laws and standards (Section 508, WCAG 2.1, CVAA), usability testing, Facebook and social media accessibility, iPhone and smartphone accessibility, universal design, and accessible images. College graduates who are well versed in web accessibility, regardless of major, will be better prepared to succeed in our high-tech, global workplace.

Getting Things Done: Writing and Designing Documents for the User Experience

Manuals, user guides, quick start guides, troubleshooting forums, how-to videos… We've all experienced the frustration of trying to use bad instructions: unclear or missing steps, ambiguous and poorly annotated images, confusing organization and page layout, error-filled writing. Developing good instructional materials requires diverse skills in writing, usability, and visual design, skills that employers value and that you will learn in this course.

In this special topics course, we will create and manipulate several objects and work with diverse materials as we learn how to write effective instructions: duct tape (for making duct tape wallets or other objects), Rubik's cubes, origami designs, Lego blocks, ropes and neckties (for tying complex knots), maps and signs, solar eclipse viewers, electronic circuits and devices, and other objects. One of your assignments will be a video-based YouTube tutorial on a software topic. Designers and writers in all disciplines need methodical approaches to creating usable documents and experiences.

Capstone: Professional ePortfolios

In this capstone course in professional writing, you will hone your job skills and create documents for success after graduation. You will reflect on the experiences and knowledge you've gained as a humanities major (and English major in particular). You will develop job application materials, including two professional ePortfolios. You'll practice answering the most common job interview questions before participating in a realistic job interview. You'll also work with a real organization, creating one or more documents to address their needs (which will satisfy the "experiential learning" course designation). Overall, the course is designed to help you reflect on where you've been academically, translate your knowledge and skills so they are legible to prospective employers, and gain experiences communicating your abilities persuasively.

Graduate

Grant and Proposal Writing

This course provides intensive practice in grant and proposal writing for students pursuing diverse funding opportunities across the sciences, humanities, and community sectors. Whether you're developing a research proposal for federal agencies (NIH, NSF), applying for humanities fellowships (NEH, ACLS), or writing foundation grants for community organizations, this course will equip you with the skills needed to compete successfully for funding.

The course begins with a foundation in plain language science and technical writing, showing you some concrete ways to translate complex ideas for diverse audiences. From there, you'll learn the fundamentals of strong proposal writing: analyzing funding landscapes and review criteria, crafting statements of need and significance, developing clear project plans and objectives, creating realistic budgets, and writing persuasively for evaluators in your field. Each of you will write one major proposal over the course of the semester, finding a real funding opportunity and putting the proposal together section by section.

The course follows a collaborative workshop model organized around disciplinary "pods" that allow for targeted instruction while maintaining shared foundational concepts. You'll build your proposal incrementally through weekly assignments, each focusing on a key component that receives feedback for revision. By regularly sharing drafts, participating in peer review, and ultimately presenting your work to a mock review panel, you'll learn to adapt your writing for the specific audiences and contexts that matter to your funding goals. By the end of the semester, you will have completed a polished, submission-ready proposal bundle along with supporting materials.

Seminar in Disability Studies: Designing Inclusive Futures

This graduate course, offered in Spring 2020, was the culmination of a funded UD Grand Challenges Interdisciplinary Grant that brought together a working group of UD faculty and graduate students committed to advancing the critical project of disability studies.

Who is part of our imagined futures? How do designers, entrepreneurs, researchers, leaders, and creators of all kinds generate possibilities for moving, interacting, sustaining, and connecting in the future? This graduate course will introduce students to disability studies and inclusive design. Throughout the semester, we'll return to the twin themes of Access and Design from multiple disciplinary perspectives.

Spring 2020—as both metaphor and reality—will be our touchstone as we reconsider the future of access: a new decade and the end of our "teen" years; a presidential election year that will offer reflections on our nation's pasts and enable choices among competing visions of the future; a census year, in which Americans will be counted and we will determine who and what counts; a medical term (20/20 vision) that crystallizes deep-seated ideas and assumptions about normalcy, perception, perfection, correction, aging, and accommodation; and a way to announce, in product development and other contexts, the new and improved model, the end of the beginning, or simply "version 2.0" (see Tara Wood et al. on "moving beyond disability 2.0").
